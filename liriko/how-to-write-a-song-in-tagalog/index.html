How to write a song in Tagalog

Paano sumulat ng kanta sa Tagalog

By Dustine Jao, maker of Liriko. Updated 27 November 2026.

Write the hook first, pick a verse–chorus shape, count syllables so each line fits the melody, rhyme at the ends of lines, and record a rough demo as soon as you have a tune.

Below: each step in detail, the two rules Filipino verse has always run on, sukat and tugma, and how they carry over to a modern song.

Eight steps

  1. Start with the line people will sing back. Write the hook before the verses: the one line that carries the song's idea, usually the title. Everything else is there to set it up.
  2. Pick a shape. A common OPM shape is verse, pre-chorus, chorus, verse, chorus, bridge, chorus. Each verse adds a detail; the chorus says the same thing every time.
  3. Count the syllables. Tagalog words grow long with affixes, so a line can outrun the melody fast. Count syllables per line and match lines that share a melody.
  4. Rhyme where the stress falls. Put rhymes at the ends of lines, and save two-syllable rhymes for the chorus, where they land hardest.
  5. Mix languages on purpose. Taglish works when the switch means something: a borrowed word that sounds right, or an English line that rhymes by sound with a Tagalog one.
  6. Use one concrete detail per verse. A jeepney seat, a text left on read, rain on a yero roof. Specific images do the feeling's work for you.
  7. Record a rough demo right away. Sing the melody into your phone the moment you have it. A melody you did not record is a melody you will not remember.
  8. Cut until every line sings. Read each line out loud at the song's tempo. Drop extra syllables, swap long affixed words for roots, and contract (ko'y, sa'yo, 'di) where it sounds natural.

Sukat: the syllable count

Filipino poetry has counted syllables for centuries. An awit, like Francisco Balagtas's Florante at Laura, runs on twelve-syllable lines; a korido, like Ibong Adarna, on eight. A song is freer than either, but the idea is the same: lines that share a melody should carry the same number of syllables, or the singer has to rush or stretch.

Tagalog makes this harder than English because affixes stack up. Mahal is two syllables; nagmamahalan is five. When a line will not fit, go back to the root word, or contract: ako ay becomes ako'y, sa iyo becomes sa'yo, hindi becomes 'di.

Tugma: the rhyme

Because Tagalog is spelled close to how it sounds, you can hear a rhyme by reading the end of the word. There are two strengths worth knowing:

RhymeWhat matchesExample
Last syllableThe final vowel and anything after itbuhay, kamay, tunay
Two syllablesThe last two syllables, from the first vowel onpangarap, sarap, harap

Last-syllable rhymes are the classic tugma and the backbone of most verses. Two-syllable rhymes, what rappers call multis, sound more deliberate; put them where you want the listener to notice, usually the chorus. Avoid rhyming a word with its own affixed form, like buhay with mabuhay: it is the same word twice, and the ear hears it.

A worked example

Say the hook is about waiting for someone who is not coming back. A first draft:

Naghihintay pa rin ako sa'yo
Kahit alam kong 'di ka na babalik

The lines do not rhyme, and the second runs long. Count: nag·hi·hin·tay pa rin a·ko sa'·yo is ten; ka·hit a·lam kong 'di ka na ba·ba·lik is eleven. Pull the second line toward a rhyme on sa'yo, which ends in -o, and trim it:

Naghihintay pa rin ako sa'yo
Kahit alam kong ika'y lumayo

Now it is ten and ten (ka·hit a·lam kong i·ka'y lu·ma·yo), and sa'yo and lumayo share their last two syllables: a strong rhyme on the line that matters.

Taglish, on purpose

Switching languages mid-song is part of how OPM sounds. It works best when the switch earns its place: an English word that sounds better on the note, or a cross-language rhyme the listener did not see coming. Rhyme by sound, not spelling: ikaw and now share the same "aw" ending when sung.

Questions

How do you write a song in Tagalog?
Start with the hook line, choose a structure (verse, pre-chorus, chorus, bridge), count syllables so lines fit the melody, rhyme at the ends of lines, add one concrete detail per verse, record a rough demo, then cut until every line sings.
What are sukat and tugma?
Sukat is the number of syllables in a line; tugma is rhyme at the ends of lines. Traditional Filipino verse fixes both: an awit such as Florante at Laura uses twelve-syllable lines, and a korido such as Ibong Adarna uses eight.
How many syllables should a line in a Tagalog song have?
As many as the melody has notes. Songs do not need a fixed count the way an awit does, but lines that share a melody should match. Liriko shows the syllable count of the line you are writing.
Can I mix English and Tagalog in a song?
Yes, and OPM does it all the time. Rhyme by sound across the two: ikaw rhymes with now. Liriko finds rhymes in both languages in the same song.
